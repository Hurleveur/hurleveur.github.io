Chakras

Chakras

Properties1
descriptionWhat the seven chakras actually are, where the system really comes from, and how the rooms of this vault ended up mapped onto them.

Sep 08, 20269 min read

The rooms of this vault are coloured by chakra — root to crown, red to violet. That mapping lives in one place, a comment in loci/quartz/static/vaultbrain.js, and until now nothing explained it. This note is the explanation, including the part most chakra material leaves out: where the system comes from, and which bits of it are ancient and which were invented in living memory.

What a chakra is supposed to be

Cakra is Sanskrit for wheel or disc. In the tantric yoga traditions that produced the idea, the chakras are not organs and were never claimed to be. They are points along a subtle body — a map laid over the physical one — used as objects of meditation. You visualise a particular centre, with its element, its syllable, its number of petals, and you work there.

The important word is used. The system is a practice instrument, not an anatomical claim. Nobody in the source texts is asserting that a spinning disc exists at the base of your spine in the way a kidney exists. They are describing where to put attention, and what happens when you do. Read that way the system stops needing to be true in a physical sense to be useful, which is also the only way it survives contact with someone who wants evidence.

The ascending order carries the actual argument: you start at survival and end at meaning. Each level is a wider circle of concern than the one below it, and each one depends on the ones under it holding. That structure is the part worth keeping even if you throw away every petal count.

Where it really comes from

This is the part that matters if you would rather not repeat something a workshop told you.

The early tantric texts do not agree on how many chakras there are. Buddhist texts from around the eighth century describe four or five. The Kubjikāmata-tantra, tenth century, gives five. Other systems count nine, twelve, more. There is no single ancient number.

The seven that everyone now recites come from one late text: the Ṣaṭ-cakra-nirūpaṇa, written by Pūrṇānanda in 1577 — sixteenth century, not antiquity. Its title means “description of the six chakras”, because the seventh, the crown, sits above and outside the series. That text reached the West through Sir John Woodroffe’s 1919 translation, published as The Serpent Power under the pen name Arthur Avalon. Essentially every Western chakra book descends from that one translation.

The rainbow is younger still, and it is not Indian at all. The traditional texts do give the chakras colours, but they are not the spectrum: the root is described with a yellow square for earth, the sacral with a white crescent for water, the navel with a red triangle for fire. Charles Leadbeater, of the Theosophical Society, published The Chakras in 1927 with colours taken from what he believed he was seeing clairvoyantly — his own invention, and not in spectral order. The clean red-orange-yellow-green-blue-indigo-violet mapping arrived with Christopher Hills’ Nuclear Evolution: Discovery of the Rainbow Body in 1977, which laid Newton’s optical spectrum over the seven centres and attached a personality type to each. It was tidy enough that nearly every book since has copied it.

So the ROYGBIV chakra chart is about fifty years old and Western. The Theosophical Society’s own magazine says so plainly, which is a decent sign it is not a hit piece.

None of that makes the system worthless. It makes it a model, assembled over centuries and finished recently, and a model is judged by whether it helps you see something you were missing — not by how old it is. Anyone selling it as unbroken five-thousand-year-old wisdom is either misinformed or counting on you being.

The seven

Each one below: the name, where it sits, what it governs, and what it looks like when that area of a life is not working. That last part is the useful half — the diagnostic, not the decoration.

1. Mūlādhāra — root. Base of the spine. Element earth, four petals, seed syllable lam. Survival, safety, money, shelter, the body, belonging somewhere. It is the ground everything else stands on. Starved, it shows up as chronic low-grade fear, hoarding, an inability to settle, or treating every ordinary problem as an emergency. Overfed, it becomes rigidity — refusing any change because the floor might move.

2. Svādhiṣṭhāna — sacral. Below the navel. Water, six petals, vam. Desire, pleasure, emotion, sex, novelty, play, and the making that comes out of all of it. This is the creative centre, and creativity here means the appetite for new experience, not skill. Starved, it looks like numbness, joylessness, a life that works on paper and tastes like nothing, or creative work that never gets started. Overfed, it is compulsion — chasing the next sensation because stopping is unbearable.

3. Maṇipūra — solar plexus. At the navel. Fire, ten petals, ram. Will, agency, discipline, the capacity to act on the world and be someone in it. Where the sacral wants, this one does. Starved: passivity, drift, other people’s plans running your week, a chronic sense of being acted upon. Overfed: domination, workaholism, force applied where persuasion would have done.

4. Anāhata — heart. Centre of the chest. Air, twelve petals, yam. Love, connection, compassion, the move from me to us. It sits deliberately in the middle: three below concerned with self, three above with something beyond it, and the heart is the hinge. Starved: isolation, transactional relationships, a sharp answer for everything and no one to call. Overfed: losing yourself in others, care that is really control, giving until there is nothing left to give from.

5. Viśuddha — throat. The throat. Element space or ether, sixteen petals, ham. Expression, truth-telling, saying the thing. Not talking — saying. Starved: swallowed words, a gap between what you think and what you tell people, resentment built out of things never said. Overfed: talking past everyone, opinions as noise, expression with nothing underneath it.

6. Ājñā — third eye. Between the eyebrows. Light rather than a physical element, two large petals, om. Insight, perception, seeing the pattern rather than the instances. Knowledge, but the kind that reorganises how you look, not the kind you accumulate. Starved: confusion, no sense of direction, reacting to events one at a time forever. Overfed: living in abstraction, endlessly theorising a life you are not in.

7. Sahasrāra — crown. Top of the head. A thousand petals, no element, no seed syllable, because it is what the others resolve into rather than another item in the list. Meaning, purpose, the sense of being part of something larger. Starved: everything works and none of it matters. Overfed — and this one is real — spiritual bypassing: using cosmic significance to skip the four lower centres, which is how people end up enlightened and unable to pay rent.

How this vault maps onto it

The seven rooms were not designed against this list — they grew, and the mapping was noticed afterward and then made explicit in the site’s colours. That is the honest order of events, and it is why some of the fits are better than others.

ChakraRoomFit
rootAlignmentgood — habits, health, routine, owning your own tools
sacralTravelstrained — the name says trips, the room now also holds the making
solar plexusWorkgood
heartFriendsgood
throatSharedgood — clippings, recommendations, what gets posted
third eyeLibrarygood
crownMeaninggood, though it absorbs anything introspective by default

Each room is the note of its own name — Alignment, Travel, Work, Friends, Shared, Library, Meaning — and each one’s own note says what it takes.

The one that strains is the sacral. Its chakra is desire, pleasure, novelty and creation; the room is called Travel, which covers the going but not the making. The creative work now sits there — which is correct by chakra and wrong by folder name.

Reading the vault as a chakra chart

The exercise in Shakra release is to find which centre is blocked. This vault is one place to look, since it is roughly a record of where attention went. Published notes per room, September 2026:

crown 71 · throat 60 · third eye 55 · root 45 · sacral 38 · solar plexus 25 · heart 17

The top three carry more than half of everything public. Read naively that is a head with a thin body, and the two thinnest are the heart and the will.

But naive is the wrong read, and this is where the exercise needs a guard. Two corrections:

  • Private is not blocked. Work has 113 notes and publishes 25. Friends has 142 and publishes 17. Those centres are not empty, they are closed — which is a completely different diagnosis, and arguably the correct behaviour for both.
  • Volume is not health. 116 of the Friends notes are imported chat exports, not written thought; the room holds 27 notes someone actually wrote. And a heart chakra measured by how much you write about friendship rather than how much you see your friends is measuring the wrong thing entirely. A journal is evidence of attention, not of living.

What does survive both corrections: the root chakra’s own body content — Alignment/Health/ — is entirely unpublished, zero notes public. The public root room is habits and philosophy with no body in it. Whatever that says, it is a real asymmetry and not an artefact of counting.

The honest version of the exercise is therefore not “count your notes”. It is: for each of the seven, name the last time you did something in that register, and notice which one takes longest to answer.
